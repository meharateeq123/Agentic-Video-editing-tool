Cleetus McFarland just crashed his Super Late Model. And instead of piling on with the critics, Dale Earnhardt Jr. did the opposite. He defended him. On air. In blunt terms. And what he said next explains why this crash might actually be good news for Cleetus's NASCAR future.
Let's break down what happened, what Dale Jr. actually said, and why this moment matters more than a single bad finish.
On August 12, Cleetus McFarland, real name Garrett Mitchell, took his Super Late Model to Berlin Raceway in Michigan for the Tekton 250 Battle at Berlin. This was his first time racing this specific car, bought for one reason: to sharpen his race craft on short tracks.
The race started well. He qualified 18th in a stacked field. For most of the night, he was running inside the top 20, close to cracking the top 15.
Then, with fewer than 20 laps to go, his car got loose. He spun, collected fellow driver Keith Herp, and both of their nights ended early. Final finish: 19th.
Cleetus didn't sugarcoat it afterward. Speaking to FloRacing, he admitted he struggled to control the car, overheating the right rear tire while trying to push too hard.
That honesty didn't stop the backlash. Online critics jumped on the crash, pointing to his earlier struggles in NASCAR's Truck Series and O'Reilly Series as proof he wasn't cut out for stock car racing.
That's where Dale Earnhardt Jr. stepped in. On a recent episode of the Dale Jr. Download podcast, he didn't just brush off the criticism, he directly called it out.
Earnhardt pointed out that Cleetus was doing exactly what the racing community had been telling him to do for months: go run short tracks, learn car control, build real race craft. He got the Late Model. He went to Berlin. He raced against tough, experienced competition. And he qualified 18th on his very first attempt.
Then came the moment that summed up his whole argument. Earnhardt said wrecking on a first attempt is nothing unusual, framing it as something almost every driver goes through early on.
His larger point: spinning out and making contact is a normal part of racing at every level, not a red flag unique to Cleetus.
Earnhardt also pushed back on the online reaction itself, saying much of the criticism ignored the obvious, that Cleetus was simply doing what people had asked him to do, and getting punished for it anyway.
Here's the part a lot of headlines missed. Berlin wasn't a one-off. Cleetus went back to the same track just days later for the first-ever event on his own Cleetus McFarland Late Model Series, a 75-lap race.
This time, the improvement Dale Jr. wanted to see actually showed up. Cleetus qualified well again and spent roughly the first 30 laps running second, before tire wear caught up with him and he faded to a 13th-place finish.
No crash. No late spin. Just a driver managing a long green-flag run and bringing the car home in one piece.
Earnhardt highlighted that second run as the real story, saying Cleetus performed impressively and was clearly enjoying the experience, texting him afterward about how much fun he was having.
Two races, back to back, at the same track. One ended in a wreck. The other ended with a top-15 finish after leading laps. That's not a driver who's out of his depth. That's a driver learning in real time, exactly the way short-track racing is supposed to work.
To understand why Dale Jr. is so patient with Cleetus, you have to look at the full picture of his racing résumé. Cleetus, a YouTube creator with millions of subscribers, only started racing ARCA in 2025. Since then, he's picked up top-10 finishes at Talladega and Charlotte, made his Truck Series debut at Daytona, and signed a part-time, multi-race deal with Richard Childress Racing in the NASCAR O'Reilly Auto Parts Series.
His O'Reilly Series debut at Rockingham in April was rough. He spun multiple times and finished six laps down. NASCAR even denied his request to run Talladega afterward, citing a lack of experience. But he was later approved for Nashville, and he keeps getting invited back, both by RCR and by other teams.
That pattern tells you something. The people actually working with Cleetus keep giving him more chances, not fewer. Team owner Mark Rette and Dale Earnhardt Jr. reportedly encouraged him to buy the Late Model in the first place, specifically so he could rack up short-track laps outside the NASCAR spotlight.
And Cleetus has been open about the long game here. His goal has never been a full-time NASCAR ride overnight. It's a step-by-step climb toward one race: the Daytona 500. Every short-track lap, every spin, every learning experience at Berlin is part of that climb.
So where does this leave Cleetus? Not as a finished product, and nobody, including Dale Jr., is claiming he is. But the criticism that followed his Berlin crash ignored the context. He crashed doing exactly what experienced racers told him to do. Days later, at the same track, he ran second for 30 laps and finished 13th.
Dale Earnhardt Jr.'s message wasn't that Cleetus is already a great short-track racer. It was simpler than that: wrecks are part of learning to race, and judging a driver on one bad night while ignoring the next good one misses the point entirely.
If Cleetus keeps stacking races like his second Berlin run on top of nights like his first, the setbacks critics pointed to could end up being exactly what turns him into the driver he's trying to become.
The road to Daytona doesn't run in a straight line. For Cleetus McFarland, right now, it's running through short tracks in Michigan, one spin and one strong run at a time.
