Denny Hamlin walked into the Chase with the biggest cushion in the field. One race later, that cushion is cut in half. A late-race hit from a car he wasn't even racing for the win turned his opening night into a damage-control mission, and now the driver chasing his first championship is already playing defense.
Here's exactly what happened at Darlington, how it happened, and why Hamlin has almost no room left for another mistake.
Before the green flag ever dropped, Hamlin looked like the strongest driver in the field. He won the regular-season championship with four wins on the year, which handed him the No. 1 seed heading into the Chase and a 25-point cushion over the second-seeded driver. In a sport where every point matters across a 10-race postseason, that kind of lead is exactly the position every driver wants going in.
And early on Sunday night, Hamlin backed it up. He started fourth and took the lead by lap 3. He finished third in Stage 1 and fourth in Stage 2, banking 15 stage points along the way. Hamlin later said his car only got stronger as the race went on. For most of the night, it looked like Hamlin wasn't just protecting his points lead. He looked ready to extend it.
The turning point wasn't a crash. It was a pit stop. During a cycle in the middle of the race, Hamlin's crew had a slow stop that dropped him from third place all the way back to roughly 20th. That single mistake put him in a completely different part of the field, surrounded by cars racing for their own finishing positions rather than a shot at the win.
Hamlin didn't dodge responsibility for what came next. "It's a professional sport. You can't have big mistakes," he said after the race. "We just had a tough pit stop there that set us back from third to about 20th, and once we got back there, you're with guys that are a little bit more aggressive and don't really care about the points."
That setup, a strong car buried in traffic because of pit road, is exactly what put Hamlin in position for the contact that followed.
Just four laps into the final stage, Hamlin got loose exiting Turn 4. He had moved from the bottom of the track to the top and didn't have a clean angle coming off the corner, forcing him to lift to keep his car off the wall. Michael McDowell, running behind him in the No. 71 Spire Motorsports Chevrolet, made contact with the back of Hamlin's car. That contact sent Hamlin's Toyota into the outside wall, right-front first.
Hamlin didn't put all the blame on McDowell. Speaking on his podcast, Actions Detrimental, he broke down his own role in the moment. "I certainly felt like I had some responsibility in it, being that I did lift off the corner because I had a moment," Hamlin said. He also said he'd spoken directly with McDowell after the race, and that McDowell didn't believe the contact was intentional, telling Hamlin he thought Hamlin would clear him without needing to slide up. Hamlin said he was giving McDowell the benefit of the doubt, calling it a racing incident he put himself into. Still, he added that knowing the outcome, he wouldn't make the same move again.
McDowell, for his part, declined to comment further when reached afterward, though he acknowledged publicly that the incident was avoidable, even while framing it as a normal part of drivers racing for their own positions.
The contact broke Hamlin's right-front toe link and damaged the right-rear control arm, serious mechanical damage for a car that had been running up front all night. Hamlin immediately radioed his crew chief, Chris Gayle, to report the toe link was gone.
What followed was a painful stretch for the No. 11 team. Hamlin nursed the damaged car around Darlington for roughly 40 laps, running more than a second and a half off the pace, waiting for a caution that would let his crew make repairs. He eventually went a full lap down before finally getting the yellow flag he needed. His team fixed the toe link during that caution, and Hamlin was able to fight his way back onto the lead lap before the race ended, salvaging an 18th-place finish instead of something worse.
Hamlin was blunt about how the night felt afterward. "Certainly not the finish we were hoping for," he said. "Just got to where our car was actually the best right before that wreck in stage three. So we never really got to see it all the way through."
Here's the number that matters most. Hamlin entered Darlington with a 25-point lead over the second-seeded driver in the Chase field. He left with just a 12-point lead, now over a new second-place driver entirely: his own Joe Gibbs Racing teammate, Christopher Bell, who won the race outright.
That's the real damage from Sunday night. Hamlin's cushion didn't just shrink. It was cut by more than half, in a single race, at the very start of a 10-race postseason where his margin for error was already thin. The rest of the standings tightened up right behind him. Ryan Blaney sits third, 16 points back. Tyler Reddick, who won Stage 1, is fourth, 24 points behind. Chase Briscoe is fifth, 37 points off the lead. Five drivers within reach of the top spot, after just one race.
This wasn't an isolated frustration for Hamlin involving Spire Motorsports. During that same Darlington race, Spire driver Carson Hocevar made contact with Brad Keselowski, spinning him into the wall and triggering a Stage 1 caution. Hamlin, speaking on his podcast about the broader pattern of incidents involving Spire cars this season, didn't hold back, criticizing the organization's approach to racing other cars. It's a signal that Hamlin sees Sunday's contact with McDowell as part of a larger frustration, not just a single unlucky moment.
Still, Hamlin was careful not to make excuses for his own team's part in it. The slow pit stop that buried him in traffic was on his own crew, not on Spire Motorsports. And the moment that triggered the contact with McDowell, sliding up the track with only half a car length of room, was a decision Hamlin owned publicly, even while noting McDowell had room to avoid it.
Hamlin is still the points leader. That part hasn't changed. But the driver with the biggest cushion in the field now has the smallest room for error of any Chase leader in recent memory this early in the postseason. A single slow pit stop and one racing incident cost him half of his advantage in one night, and the driver now breathing down his neck is his own teammate, fresh off his first win of the season.
Hamlin knows exactly what has to happen next. "We're still leading," he said. "Just gotta go to the next race track and execute at a high level and do our best to execute a much better day next week than what we did this week."
That next test comes at World Wide Technology Raceway at Gateway, where Hamlin is the defending race winner. Nine races remain to decide the 2026 champion, and after Darlington, the margin separating Hamlin from the rest of the field is thinner than it's been all year. If Sunday proved anything, it's that in the Chase, one bad pit stop and one moment off the corner is all it takes to turn a comfortable points lead into a real fight.
