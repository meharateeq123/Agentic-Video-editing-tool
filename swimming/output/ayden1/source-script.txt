Last summer, a 12-year-old broke seven national age group records in a single season. The obvious question after any run like that is simple: what happens next? Does the progress continue, or does the kid who dominated one age group get swallowed up by the next one?
Ayden Tan just answered that question. At 13 years old, he is not slowing down. He is getting faster, against tougher competition, in a completely new age bracket.
Tan aged out of the 11-12 group in September 2025, closing that chapter with twelve total National Age Group Records across two brackets, from his five records in the 10 and Under category to seven more in his final 11-12 summer.
Moving up a bracket resets the record book against him. A swimmer who dominates one age group has to prove himself all over again against boys who are often bigger, stronger, and further into puberty.
Tan wasted no time. At the Roseville Sectionals in February 2026, competing in the 50 yard backstroke, Tan did something that stopped people watching cold.
He beat an entire final of high schoolers, swimmers several years older than him, clocking 22.94 seconds. It was his first time ever under 23 seconds in that event, and it left him more than a full second ahead of the next-fastest 13-year-old backstroker in the country that season.
Two months later, at Spring Age Group Championships in April 2026, Tan lowered his 100 yard backstroke to a lifetime best of 49.25 seconds. That moved him to 18th all-time in the 13-14 boys rankings and made him the second-fastest 13-14 swimmer in the country that season.
To put the improvement in context, Tan had opened the year at 51.11 in that same event back in January 2025. He dropped nearly two full seconds in about a year, a significant improvement in an event where elite swimmers often fight for hundredths.
The backstroke progress kept climbing through the summer. By August 2026, Tan swam a 49.40 in the 100 back in Berkeley, a time that made him the fastest 13-year-old to ever swim that event.
Records at this age typically fall by tenths of a second, sometimes hundredths. Tan has been resetting the bar for his entire age group in backstroke, not just chasing it.
His improvement has not been limited to one stroke. In July 2026, at the Walnut Sectionals, Tan dropped his 100 yard freestyle to a personal best of 53.90 seconds, a full second faster than the 54.91 he had swum the previous July.
In the same meet, he lowered his 50 yard backstroke to 27.54, another second-level drop from his 28.86 the previous August.
These are not marginal gains. A full second of improvement in a single year, across multiple events, is the kind of jump coaches watch closely, because it usually means a swimmer's technique and physical development are advancing together, not just one or the other.
It is worth remembering how demanding this schedule actually is for a young athlete. During his record-breaking 11-12 summer, Tan raced in six of seven consecutive weekends.
That pace has continued into his 13-14 season, with meets stacked through the winter, spring, and summer months. Consistent racing at that frequency is unusual even among serious age-group swimmers, and it means Tan's improvements are happening under real competitive pressure, not just in isolated time trials.
What makes Tan's 13-14 season different from his record-breaking 11-12 run is the competition itself. In the younger age group, Tan was often racing against, and beating, kids his own age by wide margins.
In the 13-14 bracket, he is now racing against swimmers up to two years older, some of whom are already committing to top college programs. Beating a final of high schoolers in backstroke, as he did in February, is a different kind of statement than breaking a national age group record in a smaller local meet.
It shows he can compete directly against older, more physically developed swimmers, not just dominate his own peer group.
That distinction matters more than it might sound. Age group swimming is full of athletes who look unbeatable against their own bracket, only to find the gap close fast once they move up. Some of that is simple physics: a growth spurt for a competitor can erase a full second of advantage in a single season.
Tan's results suggest the opposite pattern so far. Rather than the field catching up to him, he has continued opening distance on his own age group while also closing the gap on swimmers who used to be well out of reach.
Tan continues to train with San Ramon Valley Aquatics in California, the same club that guided his record-breaking run the year before.
He is not the only fast swimmer in his own house. His older sister, Mikayla Tan, also swims competitively for the same club, winning events like the 200 breaststroke at recent sectional meets. Swimming families are common at the elite level, and having a sibling already navigating high-level competition can be a real advantage for a younger athlete learning to handle pressure and travel schedules.
Club stability matters more than casual fans often realize. Many young swimmers change teams and coaches repeatedly as they search for the right fit, which can disrupt technical development during exactly the years when it matters most.
Tan has stayed with the same program through his transition from the 11-12 bracket into 13-14, giving his coaches continuity in how they manage his training load, technique work, and race schedule. That kind of stability is often cited by coaches as one of the quieter factors behind a swimmer's ability to keep improving year after year, rather than plateauing after an explosive start.
The bigger question hanging over any swimmer this fast this young is what comes after age-group swimming altogether. Coaches who track long-term development often point to swimmers who show range across multiple strokes and distances as more likely to succeed at the senior level, because it suggests strong overall technique rather than one specific physical advantage.
Tan has shown that range, posting best times and records across backstroke, freestyle, butterfly, and the individual medley since he first appeared on the national radar.
There are no guarantees in age group swimming. Puberty changes bodies unpredictably. Some swimmers who dominate their early teenage years plateau once their competitors catch up physically. Others keep improving steadily into their college years and beyond.
What separates the two groups is rarely just talent. It is usually the ability to keep improving technically even as the competition gets tougher and the margins get smaller.
Tan's own numbers make the case for optimism. A swimmer whose times drop by full seconds year over year, across backstroke, freestyle, and the medley, is showing the kind of broad-based development that tends to carry forward rather than stall out.
Compare that to a swimmer who only improves in one narrow event. A one-event specialist can be caught quickly once puberty changes body proportions. A swimmer improving everywhere at once has more paths to keep climbing even if one particular stroke stalls temporarily.
So far, that is exactly what Tan has done. He did not just hold onto his 11-12 form after aging up. He beat older competition in finals, broke into the top 20 all-time in his new age group, and kept dropping full seconds off his personal bests across multiple events, all within his first year in the 13-14 bracket.
The pattern so far suggests Tan is not just a swimmer who peaked early. He is a swimmer whose results keep climbing every time the competition gets harder. That is the trait that separates age group record holders who fade from the ones who go on to make senior national teams.
