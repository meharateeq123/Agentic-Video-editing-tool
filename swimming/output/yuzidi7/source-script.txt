Four tenths of a second. That is the entire gap between the fastest 200 individual medley ever swum by a woman, and the second fastest. The swimmer who holds that record is 19 years old, a three-time Olympic champion, chasing history in her prime. The swimmer sitting four tenths behind her is 13.
Her name is Yu Zidi. At the 2026 Asian Games, she did not just close in on one of the biggest names in swimming. She walked away with three individual gold medals and made it impossible to ignore what happens if she keeps improving at this rate.
Start with what actually happened in Tokyo. Between September 20 and September 23, 2026, Yu Zidi won gold in three different events: the 200 meter butterfly, the 200 meter individual medley, and the 400 meter individual medley. All three came with Asian Games records attached. The 200 IM swim also carried an Asian record and a world junior record.
Look closer at each result, because the story is not the same in every event. In the 200 fly, Yu touched in 2:05.08. In the 400 IM, she went 4:28.56, becoming the fifth-fastest performer in history and just missing Ye Shiwen's 2012 Olympic continental record by 0.13 seconds. Both are extraordinary swims for a 13-year-old. But the one that changed the conversation entirely was the 200 IM.
Yu swam 2:06.10 in that final. That time made her the second-fastest woman to ever swim the event, trailing only one name in history: Summer McIntosh, at 2:05.70. A gap of 0.40 seconds separates a 13-year-old from the fastest 200 IM ever recorded.
To understand how unusual that is, you need to know exactly who Summer McIntosh is. She is not a fading record holder waiting to be caught. She is 19 years old, the reigning Olympic champion in the 200 IM, and currently owns four individual long course world records at once: the 200 IM, the 400 IM, the 400 freestyle, and, as of July 2026, the 200 meter butterfly, a mark that had stood since 2009 before she broke it. McIntosh is not standing still. She is actively rewriting the record book across multiple events in the same season that Yu Zidi is chasing her.
That is what makes the 200 IM gap so remarkable. Yu is not closing in on an old record nobody defends anymore. She is within striking distance of a time set by a swimmer who is simultaneously one of the most dominant active athletes in the sport.
The 200 IM swim in Tokyo also had its own drama attached. Yu's own teammate, Yu Yiting, had out-qualified her in the morning heats by more than half a second and looked ready to defend her Asian title. By the time the final ended, Yu Zidi had beaten her by 1.89 seconds. Whatever adjustment she made between the morning and the evening, it was enough to turn a half-second deficit into a two-second win, against a fellow national record holder, not an easy field.
Now compare the other two events where their careers overlap, because the picture changes. In the 400 IM, McIntosh's world record sits at 4:23.65, set in June 2025. Yu's 4:28.56 leaves a gap of nearly five seconds, a real distance at this level. In the 200 fly, McIntosh's world record stands at 2:01.65. Yu's 2:05.08 leaves a gap of more than three seconds. In both of those events, Yu is fast, historically fast for her age, but nowhere near touching the record.
That contrast matters, because it tells you exactly where this rivalry is real right now, and where it is still speculation. The 200 IM gap of 0.40 seconds is genuinely close. The 400 IM and 200 fly gaps are not. What makes Yu's Asian Games campaign significant is not that she is about to catch McIntosh in every event. It is that she is fast across all three of McIntosh's signature events at once, at an age when most swimmers have not even settled on their best distance yet.
That range is the real story. McIntosh built her career on being dangerously versatile across freestyle, butterfly, and the medley, a combination almost no other swimmer in history has managed at an elite level. Yu Zidi's three golds in Tokyo show early signs of that same range. She did not win one event and struggle in the others. She won three, each in a different discipline, each with a record attached.
McIntosh's own progression in the 400 IM shows exactly how fast a record can move once a dominant swimmer locks onto an event. She broke that world record in 2023 at 4:25.87, then lowered it again in 2024 to 4:24.38, then again in June 2025 to its current mark of 4:23.65, setting a new world record in that event for three straight years at her national trials. If Yu Zidi's 400 IM follows even a fraction of that same trajectory, the nearly five-second gap that looks large today could shrink considerably faster than most people expect.
Consider the timeline each swimmer is working with. McIntosh is already in her physical prime, likely to remain there through the Los Angeles Olympics in 2028, when she will be 21. Yu Zidi will be 15 at those same Games, still years away from her physical peak. If Yu's current improvement curve continues even partially, the 200 IM gap of 0.40 seconds is the kind of margin that can close in a single strong season. It is also the kind of margin that can hold for years if McIntosh keeps improving at the same pace she showed in 2026, when she broke world records in three different events within a matter of days.
McIntosh herself is not standing still while all of this plays out. She trains under Bob Bowman, the coach who guided Michael Phelps through his entire career, after moving to Texas in 2025 specifically to work with him. Her 2026 season alone produced world records in the 200 fly, plus the fastest 400 free and third-fastest 800 free swims of her career, on top of her existing 200 and 400 IM records. Whatever Yu Zidi is chasing, she is chasing a moving target.
There is a reason this comparison is being taken seriously rather than dismissed as hype around a young prodigy. Yu Zidi's rise has been consistent, not a single lucky swim. She became the youngest medalist in World Aquatics Championship history in 2025, then lowered her personal bests across the 200 IM, 400 IM, and 200 fly in the months that followed. Her time in Tokyo did not come out of nowhere. It came at the end of a steady progression that has continued for more than a year.
The Olympic path ahead adds another layer to this story. Los Angeles 2028 sits just under two years away. Before that, the 2027 World Championships in Budapest offer the first realistic chance for Yu and McIntosh to race each other directly in a major final, assuming both compete in the same events. That meet would answer a question nobody can answer from results alone: how does a 13-year-old who has only raced at continental level handle lining up next to the world's best in a true global final.
None of this guarantees a rivalry actually forms. McIntosh has years of experience at the highest level of pressure racing that Yu has not yet faced. A 0.40 second gap in one event does not predict what happens across three events, at multiple championships, over two more years of physical development for both swimmers. Yu could plateau, as many fast teenagers do once their competitors catch up physically. McIntosh could extend her records even further, keeping the gap the same size or growing it.
What is not in question is the shape of what just happened. A 13-year-old finished her Asian Games campaign with three individual gold medals, three Games records, and a 200 IM time that put her within four tenths of a second of the fastest swim in the history of the event. The swimmer who owns that record is not slowing down, not standing still, and not an easy target. She is the best in the world right now, in four different events, and a teenager just showed she can already swim in the same range.
