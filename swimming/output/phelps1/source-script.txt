Twenty-eight Olympic medals. Twenty-three of them gold. More than any athlete in the history of the Games. Then, in 2016, Michael Phelps walked out of the pool for the last time as a competitor. What happened after that is a story most fans never see.

This is what life actually looks like for the greatest swimmer of all time, nearly a decade after he stopped racing.

Phelps retired following the 2016 Rio Olympics with 39 world records to his name, a career that started at age 15 in Sydney and ended at 31 in Rio.

Start with where he lives. Phelps and his family are based in Paradise Valley, Arizona, a wealthy suburb near Scottsdale, sitting in the shadow of Camelback Mountain. He first moved there in December 2015, buying a 6,010 square foot home for 2.5 million dollars so he could train alongside his longtime coach Bob Bowman, who had taken a position at Arizona State University. That original house had five bedrooms, six and a half bathrooms, a media room, and a wine room built for entertaining.

Phelps later sold that property and upgraded within the same area. Multiple real estate reports place his current Paradise Valley home in the five to six million dollar range, featuring mountain views, a private pool, and the kind of quiet luxury that fits a man who says he prefers family time over flash. He never left Arizona after his coaching career there began, and today the state is simply home.

Money is naturally part of this story. Estimates of Phelps's net worth vary depending on the source, ranging from around 80 million to over 100 million dollars. That fortune was never built primarily on Olympic prize money. USA Swimming bonuses for medals, even for a haul as historic as his, only account for a small fraction of his wealth. The real engine has always been endorsements.

To put that prize money in perspective, his eight gold medals at the Beijing Olympics in 2008 earned him roughly 2 million dollars in performance bonuses from USA Swimming and sponsors. Across his entire Olympic career, medal bonuses and national rewards are estimated to have added up to somewhere around 7 million dollars total. That is a serious sum, but it is a small fraction of the fortune endorsements delivered over the same period.

At the height of his competitive career, Phelps was earning well over 10 million dollars a year through sponsorship deals with brands like Under Armour, Omega, Visa, Subway, and Speedo. His partnership with Speedo alone, tied to his signature racing goggles, brought in significant licensing income for years. Even after retirement, many of these relationships continued in modified forms, shifting from pure athletic endorsement toward brand ambassador and advisory roles.

But the most interesting part of Phelps's post-retirement life is not the money. It is what he chose to spend his time on.

In 2018, Phelps partnered with Talkspace, an online therapy company, joining its advisory board and becoming one of the most recognizable faces in the push to normalize mental health treatment. This was not a random celebrity endorsement. Phelps has spoken publicly and repeatedly about his own struggles with depression, including a period after the 2012 Olympics when he has said he considered ending his life. In interviews, he has described mental health advocacy as work that means more to him than his medal count.

At the HIMSS Global Health Conference in 2022, Phelps told the audience that when people ask what he does now, his answer is simple: mental health. That is the work. His nonprofit, the Michael Phelps Foundation, began by teaching children water safety and swimming skills, then expanded its mission to include mental health resources for young people. In 2024, he signed a new multi-year partnership with Talkspace worth a reported 3 million dollars, extending that advocacy work across social media, podcasts, and Olympic-themed campaigns.

His business interests outside advocacy have stayed close to the sport that made him famous. Phelps has a long-running partnership with Aqua Sphere on a line called MP by Michael Phelps, designing swimwear, goggles, and training gear for competitive and recreational swimmers. It is a brand built on decades of firsthand knowledge of what a serious swimmer actually needs in the water, rather than a name simply licensed for a paycheck.

That kind of brand extension is common among retired Olympic champions, but few have sustained it as long as Phelps has. His name recognition has stayed strong for nearly a decade after his final race, partly because he has continued showing up as a commentator and analyst during Olympic broadcasts, offering technical breakdowns of races most viewers would otherwise not understand. That role keeps him connected to the sport without the pressure of competition, and it keeps his name in front of a new generation of swimming fans who never saw him race in person.

He also stayed connected to competitive swimming itself. Phelps has worked as a volunteer assistant coach for the Arizona State University swim team alongside Bob Bowman, the same coach who guided him through his entire competitive career. That relationship, spanning more than two decades, is rare in elite sports, where athletes and coaches often part ways once competition ends.

Family life sits at the center of everything else. Phelps married Nicole Johnson, a former Miss California USA, in a private ceremony in June 2016 that was not publicly reported until months later. Together they have four sons: Boomer, born in 2016, Beckett, born in 2018, Maverick, born in 2019, and Nico, born in 2024. The family has remained largely private about day-to-day life, but Phelps has spoken often about fatherhood reshaping his priorities after a career that once revolved entirely around the black line at the bottom of a pool.

Phelps has described parenting as its own kind of demanding training block, one where the payoff is not measured in seconds but in showing up consistently for four young kids. That framing matters for a man who spent nearly two decades measuring his worth in hundredths of a second. Trading a stopwatch for a school pickup line is its own adjustment, and Phelps has been candid that it took real effort to redefine success once competitive swimming stopped providing the scoreboard.

His vehicles reflect the same theme as his home: comfortable rather than flashy. Reports describe a Cadillac Escalade and a BMW 7 Series among his cars, high-end but far from the exotic supercar collections some retired athletes accumulate. Phelps has also invested in his own physical recovery, reportedly building a home wellness setup that includes cold plunge and infrared sauna equipment, tools that reflect an athlete's mindset toward long-term health even after competition ends.

What makes Phelps's post-retirement life stand out is not the size of his house or the models of his cars. It is the shift in what he considers his real work. For eighteen years, his identity was built entirely around being the fastest man in the water. Today, by his own account, his focus is on being present for his sons, running a foundation aimed at kids, and using his platform to make it easier for people to say they are struggling and ask for help.

Phelps built one of the greatest athletic careers in history through obsessive discipline and years of five a.m. training sessions. What he built after that career is a quieter kind of success: a stable family life, a business built around the sport he loves, and an advocacy platform he has said matters more to him than any of his 28 medals.
