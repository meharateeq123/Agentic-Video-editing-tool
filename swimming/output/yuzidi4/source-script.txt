The morning session ends, and the fastest qualifier for the 200 meter individual medley final is not Yu Zidi. It is her own teammate, Yu Yiting, the defending Asian champion, more than half a second ahead of her. For a few hours, it looks like the 13-year-old's win in the 200 fly the day before might have been the peak of her Asian Games. Then the final starts.
By the time the race ends, the scoreboard shows a time nobody in that pool expected. 2 minutes 6.10 seconds. An Asian record. A world junior record. The second-fastest 200 IM ever swum by any woman in history. And a margin of just four tenths of a second between a 13-year-old and the fastest 200 IM in the world.
This is the story of that race, swum on September 21, 2026, at the Tokyo Aquatics Centre, and why it changed how the swimming world talks about Yu Zidi.
China's own record book tells part of the story here too. Before this race, the national and Asian standard in the 200 IM had already changed hands twice within the same year. Yu Yiting set it at 2:06.82 back in June 2026. That mark lasted three months before her own teammate took it away in Tokyo by more than seven tenths of a second, a significant jump for a record that had barely settled in.
Start with what happened in the water, stroke by stroke. Yu Zidi attacked the opening butterfly leg immediately, splitting 26.91 seconds. That is faster than Summer McIntosh's own world-record pace on that same leg, which sits at 27.19. A 13-year-old was ahead of the fastest 200 IM ever swum after just one length.
The backstroke leg is where the gap opened up. Yu split 31.96 seconds, putting her at 58.87 at the halfway mark, but that was behind McIntosh's world-record checkpoint of 57.99. Backstroke remains the one leg where Yu still trails the world record holder by a real margin, and analysts who track her progress have noted that this is the clearest technical gap left in her race.
Then came the breaststroke leg, and it changed everything. Yu split 37.04 seconds, a leg strong enough to pull her back within sixteen hundredths of McIntosh's own breaststroke pace. Few 200 IM swimmers in history have been able to match elite breaststroke speed on top of elite butterfly speed. Yu did both in the same race.
She closed with a 30.19 freestyle split, just slightly slower than McIntosh's closing 29.95. Final time: 2:06.10. It broke Yu Yiting's Asian record of 2:06.82 by 0.72 seconds. It broke the Asian Games record. It broke the world junior record. And it moved Yu past Katinka Hosszu's 2:06.12, a time that had itself been the world record before McIntosh broke it, pushing Hosszu down to third all-time. Yu Zidi is now the second-fastest woman to ever swim the 200 meter individual medley. Only Summer McIntosh, at 2:05.70, has gone faster.
The margin between them is 0.40 seconds. In a race that takes over two minutes to complete, four tenths of a second is a fraction most viewers could not perceive with the naked eye.
Consider what that time means outside the record book, too. Yu's 2:06.10 was faster than the time Summer McIntosh swam to win Olympic gold in this exact event at the 2024 Paris Games, where McIntosh touched in 2:06.56. A 13-year-old, racing at a continental championship, just swam faster than the reigning Olympic champion's own gold-medal-winning time in that same event.
Look closer at what the splits actually reveal about her race shape. Most 200 IM specialists build their advantage in one or two legs and try to survive the rest. McIntosh's own world record is built on a strong opening fly and a dominant closing freestyle, with back and breast as connecting legs. Yu's swim followed a different pattern. She went out even faster than McIntosh on the opening fifty, then gave back time on backstroke, before producing a breaststroke split that nearly matched the world record pace exactly. That kind of balance, being competitive on three different strokes rather than dominant on just one, is rare at any age, let alone 13.
There is also the prelims twist to consider. Yu Yiting, the swimmer who out-qualified Yu Zidi that morning by more than half a second, ended up finishing 1.89 seconds behind her in the final. A winning margin that large is unusual in an event where elite finalists are typically separated by tenths of a second, not full seconds. Whatever happened between the morning heats and the evening final, Yu Zidi found another gear that her own training partner and defending champion teammate simply could not match. Japan's Mio Narita took bronze.
This was not an isolated swim. It was Yu's second gold medal in as many days. The night before, she had already won the 200 meter butterfly in 2:05.08, breaking Zhang Yufei's Asian Games record and finishing more than three seconds clear of the field. That swim alone made her the 14th-fastest performer in history in that event. Her own reaction to it was almost as notable as the time itself. She called the performance "so-so" and immediately started listing technical details she still wanted to fix. That is not the reaction of a swimmer satisfied with being fast. It is the reaction of someone already thinking about the next tenth of a second.
Put both swims together, and the picture becomes clear. In two days at these Asian Games, Yu Zidi has shown world-class speed in butterfly, backstroke, breaststroke, and freestyle, the four disciplines that make up the medley. The 200 IM specifically demands elite technique across all of them within a single race, with no room to hide a weak leg. Her time of 2:06.10 is currently the fastest 200 IM swum by anyone in the world this year, at any age, in any country.
That opening night also set the tone for how dominant China was at this meet. The team won six of seven swimming gold medals on the first night of competition alone, with Yu's butterfly title as the individual highlight. But where the rest of that gold medal haul was part of a larger national medal count, Yu's second win in the 200 IM stood on its own as a singular, record-breaking performance that pulled attention away from the team result entirely.
What happens next follows quickly. Yu still has the 400 meter individual medley left to swim later this week at these same Asian Games, an event where she already ranks eighth all-time after a 4:30.79 swim earlier this year. If her 200 IM form carries over, that race could produce another headline before the meet even ends.
Beyond Tokyo, the bigger picture points toward a potential head-to-head with Summer McIntosh herself. The two swimmers' event programs overlap almost completely: the 200 butterfly, the 200 individual medley, and the 400 individual medley. McIntosh is the reigning Olympic champion in all three. That collision course could arrive as early as the 2027 World Championships in Budapest, where Yu, still only 14 at that point, could face McIntosh directly in a major final for the first time. Beyond that sits the Los Angeles 2028 Olympics, still nearly two years away, where Yu would be 15.
None of this guarantees anything. Swimming has seen fast teenagers before who never closed the final gap to a world record, and McIntosh herself is still only 20, with years of her own competitive prime still ahead of her. A four-tenths gap can shrink or it can hold for years, depending on how both swimmers develop.
The backstroke leg remains the clearest place to look for where that gap could close. Yu already matches or beats world-record pace on fly and breaststroke. If her backstroke speed continues improving at the same rate her other legs have, the four tenths separating her from McIntosh's record could come from that single fifty meters alone. That is the specific technical detail worth watching the next time she races this event.
What is not in question is what happened in that pool in Tokyo. A 13-year-old went into a final as the second-fastest qualifier, trailed her own teammate through the morning, and then produced a swim fast enough to beat an Olympic champion's own gold-medal time, in an event where only one woman on the planet has ever gone faster.
